GPT-5.3-Codex-Spark: What's New, How to Access It, What It Costs

GPT-5.3-Codex-Spark is a research preview built for real-time coding: past 1,000 tokens per second, 128k of context, text only, and ChatGPT Pro at the door.

The short versionSpark is for developers who work in tight loops inside a repository they already understand, where waiting thirty seconds for a response is the actual bottleneck. It is not for long autonomous runs, not for anything involving images, and not for people who want a model that quietly verifies its own work, because it deliberately does less of that. The caveat that decides whether it fits: 128k of context and text-only input at launch, plus a research preview gated to ChatGPT Pro. If your problem is that there is no project to iterate on yet, a faster model will not help, and Begin.sh will hand you a working static site or Expo app from a prompt.

A smaller model, shipped for one reason

OpenAI released GPT-5.3-Codex-Spark as a research preview on February 12, 2026, describing it as a smaller version of GPT-5.3-Codex and its first model designed for real-time coding. It is also the first product of the Cerebras partnership announced the previous month, which Simon Willison noted was only four weeks earlier, an unusually short gap between announcement and shipped integration. The claim is more than 1,000 tokens per second while staying capable on real coding work; Cerebras, writing about its own hardware, puts the figure above 1,200. OpenAI is explicit about why it is a preview rather than a launch: it wants developer feedback while Cerebras ramps datacenter capacity and while the end-to-end experience gets hardened. That framing is worth taking at face value.

The limits stated at launch

Four constraints matter and all four come from OpenAI directly. The context window is 128k, which is small next to the frontier models people use for repository-wide work. The model is text-only, so screenshots, diagrams and design mocks are out. Access during the research preview is for ChatGPT Pro users. And Spark carries its own rate limits, with usage not counting against your standard limits, which is generous, though OpenAI also warns that under high demand you may hit limited access or temporary queuing. On price, the honest answer is that no rate was published at launch. Simon Willison, who had preview access, wrote plainly that it was not yet clear what pricing would look like, so treat any specific figure elsewhere as unverified.

Why it does less on purpose

Spark's default behaviour is not a trimmed-down accident, it is a design decision. OpenAI says the model keeps a lightweight working style: it makes minimal, targeted edits, and it does not automatically run tests unless you ask it to. Read that as a contract rather than a shortcoming. The frontier models are tuned for long autonomous runs where checking their own work matters because nobody is watching. Spark assumes you are watching, and OpenAI describes interrupting and redirecting mid-task as a core interaction pattern. Codex now spans two modes as a result, a deep mode for large prompts and long-running tasks, and a fast mode for rapid iterative collaboration. Choosing the wrong one for the job is the main way people come away unimpressed.

How people actually work with it

Cerebras published a practical playbook and its first rule is the least obvious: treat Spark like a pair programmer, not a delegate. Focus on one thing at a time, stay in the loop, and resist switching to another window while it runs, because the moment you leave you have given up the only advantage. Steering is short and direct in practice, along the lines of only change this file, do not touch types yet, or show me the diff before continuing. The second rule follows from the speed: validation becomes cheap. Pre-commit hooks, a full test suite between tasks, linting and type checks, browser-based QA and diff reviews all stop being a tax on the cycle when generation is near-instant, so build tests first and loop until they pass.

Speed is not the same as quality

Worth keeping in view before switching everything over. Simon Willison ran the same prompt through Spark and through regular GPT-5.3 Codex at medium effort, confirmed Spark was significantly faster, and reported that the slower model produced the better result. His conclusion was that the interesting property here is not quality but the flow state that arrives when a model answers fast enough to keep iterating. That is a real productivity gain and it is not a benchmark win. So use Spark where the loop is the point and reach for the bigger model where the answer is. Getting useful output in four moves

  1. Check you have ChatGPT Pro
    The research preview is offered to Pro users. Without it the model will not appear, which reads like a rollout delay but is an access tier.
  2. Pick fast mode deliberately
    Codex now spans a deep mode for large prompts and long tasks and a fast mode for iterative work. Spark belongs to the second; sending it a sprawling brief wastes it.
  3. Steer in short sentences
    Only change this file. Do not touch types yet. Show me the diff before continuing. Interrupting and redirecting mid-task is the intended interaction, not a workaround.
  4. Ask for the tests explicitly
    It will not run them on its own. Since generation is near-instant, running the full suite between tasks costs little, so make it part of the loop rather than a final step.
